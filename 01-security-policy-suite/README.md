# 01. Information Security Policy Suite

**File:** [Westbridge-CC-Information-Security-Policy-Suite.docx](./Westbridge-CC-Information-Security-Policy-Suite.docx) (24 pages)

A committee report seeking adoption of four information security policies, with the policies
attached as appendices.

## Context

The council's existing Information Security Policy was approved in July 2021. It is eleven
pages, written against ISO/IEC 27001:2013, which was withdrawn in October 2025. In the five
years since, the council moved its collaboration estate to Microsoft 365, stood up an Azure
landing zone, adopted 62 SaaS products and made hybrid working standard for 2,900 staff. The
policy mentions none of that, and it predates generative AI entirely.

The gap analysis found nineteen remediation actions that cannot start until the council has
approved policies setting the standard those actions are meant to reach. This document is the
first deliverable of that programme.

## Approach

The thing that makes this artefact different from a policy template is the first six pages. A
policy that has not been adopted is not a policy, and in local government adoption happens
through a committee report that has to survive scrutiny by elected members who are not
technical.

So the report is written the way UK council committee reports are actually written: purpose,
recommendations, background, options appraisal, and then implications broken out by finance,
legal, human resources, equalities, risk and climate. Each implication is signed off by the
officer who owns it, and the sign-off table carries real dates. The s151 Officer confirms no
in-year budget pressure. The Monitoring Officer confirms consistency with the Constitution. The
trade unions were consulted, raised two points, and both were accepted into the policies.

I wrote four separate policies rather than one combined document on purpose, and the report says
why. A single eleven-page policy covering every subject gets read once at induction and never
again. Four short topic-specific policies can be issued to the audiences that need them, revised
independently as technology moves, and cited precisely when someone has to justify a decision.

## What is in it

The committee report runs to seven sections including a three-option appraisal and the
implications table.

Appendix A, Acceptable Use Policy. Covers scope across employees, contractors, volunteers and
elected members. Section 7 deals with generative AI: six rules on what may not be entered into
these tools, a prohibition on using them to make decisions about individuals without a DPIA, and
a requirement to disclose AI assistance where a reader would expect to know. Section 8 sets out
what monitoring happens, who authorises examination of an individual's activity, and that the
count of such authorisations is reported annually to committee.

Appendix B, Incident Response Policy. A four-tier severity model with response times attached.
Seven named roles with defined authority, including the Incident Manager's power to disconnect
systems without further approval. Five response stages. The requirement to preserve evidence
before rebuilding exists because of the March 2026 incident, and the policy says so.

Appendix C, Access Control Policy. Six principles, then sections on identity, authentication,
privileged access, granting and changing access, and recertification. The recertification table
sets frequency by data classification, quarterly for special category data down to annually for
OFFICIAL. Internal movers are treated as a leaver and a joiner, which is the single change most
likely to be resisted and the one that matters most.

Appendix D, Data Classification and Handling Policy. Four tiers based on the Government Security
Classifications, extended to separate personal data from special category data. The handling
matrix sets fifteen requirements across all four tiers, covering storage, email, bulk transfer,
removable media, printing, AI tools, test environments, retention and disposal.

## What I would do differently

The AI section will date fastest. It is written against tools as they behave now, and the rules
about not entering personal data assume a clear boundary between approved and unapproved tools
that is already blurring as AI features appear inside products the council already licenses. On
a second pass I would write it as a decision test rather than a list of prohibitions.

The handling matrix is also close to the limit of what fits on a page. Fifteen rows by four
columns is readable in Word and would not be on a phone, and most staff will meet this policy on
a phone.
